Step 01 · Finances

The Mortgage Process

Step one is getting a pre-approval letter from a lender stating how much you're qualified for. A pre-approval is valid for 60–90 days, so we time it for when you're ready to actively shop.

Don't understand something your lender says? Stop and ask. This is your home buying journey, and you deserve to understand every step. If your lender makes you feel rushed or talked down to, that's your sign to interview a different one.
Today's rates and creative financing

Rates have stayed elevated compared to the 2020–2022 era. The good news: sellers are far more open to concessions, rate buy-downs (2-1 or 3-2-1), and closing cost help. We'll structure your offer to take advantage of where the market actually is, not where it was three years ago.

5 Questions to Ask Every Lender

  1. What type of loan do you recommend for me, and why? There's no universally "best" mortgage. Just the best one for your situation. Conventional, FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo: each has tradeoffs. Make sure they explain why their pick fits you.
  2. What's the minimum down payment, and how does it change with the loan type? Down payments range from 0% (VA/USDA) to 20%+ (jumbo or to avoid PMI). Be honest about what you have on hand. There are programs you may not know exist.
  3. What's the interest rate AND the APR? Everyone quotes the rate. The APR includes lender fees and is the truer comparison number. Ask for both.
  4. Can I lock the rate? For how long, and what does it cost? If rates are climbing, locking can save thousands. Some lenders offer free 30–60 day locks; longer locks usually cost extra.
  5. What will my closing costs be, and can they be financed or covered by seller credits? Closing costs typically run 2–5% of the loan amount. We can often negotiate for the seller to cover some or all of them.
  6. Do you offer rate buy-downs, and is it worth it for my scenario? A 2-1 buy-down lowers your rate by 2% in year one, 1% in year two, then settles at your full rate. Often paid by the seller as a concession, and a powerful tool in today's market.
First-Time Buyer · Virginia

Money on the table for first-time buyers

Virginia has some of the strongest first-time buyer help in the country, and most of it runs through Virginia Housing (the state housing finance agency). Your lender applies for these, not you, so the first move is choosing a Virginia Housing-approved lender.

Start Here
Virginia Housing Down Payment Assistance Grant

A true grant you never repay: up to 2% of the purchase price with a Virginia Housing conventional loan, or 2.5% with a Virginia Housing FHA loan. You bring at least 1% down, and the grant covers the rest of the down payment. You must be a first-time buyer (or buying in an Area of Economic Opportunity), use an eligible Virginia Housing bond loan, and fall within household income and sales price limits. It can be paired with assistance from outside Virginia Housing, including the local programs below.

  1. Virginia Housing Plus Second Mortgage A 30-year fixed second loan that covers the down payment, so you can buy with 0% down (Virginia Housing lets qualified buyers borrow up to 1.5% more than the sales price). Open to first-time and repeat buyers, with higher income limits than the grant. It is a loan, not a gift, so it adds a second monthly payment, and it can't be combined with the grant. Best fit if your income is over the grant limit.
  2. DHCD Down Payment & Closing Cost Assistance (through local programs) The Virginia Department of Housing and Community Development funds help that is delivered by local governments and nonprofit housing agencies, not by DHCD directly. Two programs:
    HOMEownership DPA: first-time buyers at or below 80% of area median income (AMI). Up to 10% of the sales price (15% in DHCD-approved areas) plus up to $2,500 toward closing costs, capped at $40,000. Structured as a deferred second loan.
    Virginia Pilot DPA: first-time buyers at or below 60% of AMI. Up to $50,000, forgivable over 15 years.
    Both expect a small contribution from you (up to 1% of the price) and homebuyer education. Processing runs through the local agency and takes longer than a grant, so we start the application before we write an offer.
  3. Virginia Housing Closing Cost Assistance Grant A true grant for first-time buyers using a Virginia Housing VA or RHS (USDA) bond loan. It can reduce or eliminate the cash you need for closing costs. Income limits apply.
  4. HOME of Virginia: homebuyer education and counseling Housing Opportunities Made Equal of Virginia (HOME) is a Richmond nonprofit offering free one-on-one pre-purchase counseling, group homebuyer classes, money management, and credit recovery classes. It serves Richmond, Henrico, Chesterfield, Hanover, Goochland, Powhatan, Colonial Heights, and Hopewell. HOME also helps eligible buyers access down payment and closing cost assistance (household income at or below 80% of AMI and at least $40,000 a year). Some assistance programs require this kind of education and counseling before closing, so start early: apply online at homeofva.org, allow up to 10 business days for review, then complete the education course and a counseling appointment.
  5. Virginia Housing free homebuyer class Required for Virginia Housing loans, free, in person or online. It covers credit, budgeting, lenders, inspections, and closing. Take it now, before we find the house, so it never holds up your loan.
  6. Virginia First-Time Home Buyer Savings Account Still saving? Designate a savings or investment account as a first-time home buyer account and the interest and gains come off your Virginia taxable income. Limits: $50,000 of principal and $150,000 of principal plus interest per account. The money must go to the down payment or closing costs, or the tax break is paid back. Ask your tax preparer to set it up on your Virginia return.

Before We Tour

Your buyer agreement and how I'm paid

With your financing and assistance lined up, the last step before we tour is putting our working relationship in writing. Here's what that means and why it protects you.

Virginia Law
A written buyer agreement comes first

Virginia law requires a written buyer brokerage agreement before I show you a home (Va. Code §54.1-2132 and §54.1-2137). It spells out my services, the term, and how I'm paid, and we'll walk through every line together before you sign.

Buyer-side compensation is no longer assumed

Sellers are no longer required to advertise compensation to a buyer's agent. In most deals, we still negotiate for the seller to cover it as part of the offer, but if they won't, we have options (concessions, financing it into your loan, etc.). I'll never let this become a surprise.

Step 02 · Home Search

Searching strategically

Before we tour anything, I'll have you build two lists: Needs (non-negotiables) and Wants (nice-to-haves). It saves us months of touring the wrong houses.

Needs might look like

  • Enough square footage for your household (now and 5 years out)
  • Right number of bedrooms and bathrooms
  • Main-floor primary suite (or single-story)
  • Reasonable commute to work / school district
  • Attached garage
  • Fenced yard for kids or pets
  • Within your real budget after rate, taxes, insurance, HOA

Wants might look like

  • Specific paint color or exterior style
  • Pool, hot tub, or other water feature
  • Specific carpet, hardwood, or tile
  • Updated kitchen (quartz counters, gas range, etc.)
  • Walk-in closet, double vanity, soaking tub
  • View, mountain access, walkability
You can't change the lot, the location, or the price you paid. Spend more time on those three than on anything else.

Tips for searching online

You already know the sites: Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com. Here's how to actually use them well:

  • Use filters, but loosely. Tight filters cut you off from "the one." Set max price slightly above your real cap, since homes often sell under ask, especially with concessions.
  • Pull up Google Street View on every listing. Photos are heavily edited. Street View shows the neighbors, the road, the actual surroundings.
  • Check the property's history. Sold three times in five years? Big price drop? Worth asking why before we tour.
  • Send me MLS numbers and addresses. I'll call the listing agents to gauge motivation, get pre-inspection info, and check for offer deadlines.

Making the most of showings

  • Re-read your needs/wants list before each showing. Keeps you objective when emotions run high.
  • Take photos and short videos. By house #4 they all blur together.
  • Don't get distracted by staging. Furniture and paint go away. Layout, light, and lot don't.
  • Open every closet. Storage matters more than buyers expect.
  • Run the water, flush toilets, test light switches. You'd be surprised what gets caught.
  • Take your time. If a home makes a strong first impression, tell me. I'll have us linger.
Step 03 · Under Contract

Making an offer that lands

You've found "the one." Now let's craft an offer that gets accepted, without overpaying or stripping out protections you actually need.

What makes an offer stand out

  • A current pre-approval letter. Signals you're serious, qualified, and ready to close.
  • Strong (not necessarily highest) price. I pull comps and read the seller's motivation. Sometimes a clean offer at list beats a higher offer with a long contingency list.
  • Larger earnest money deposit. Shows skin in the game. We use the inspection contingency to protect it.
  • Cash, if you have it, say so. No financing risk = less to worry about for the seller. We can still recommend an inspection.
  • Quick close. 21–25 days reads as "professional" to listing agents. We only commit to it if your lender can deliver.
  • Smart contingencies, not no contingencies. Inspection, financing, and appraisal contingencies exist to protect you. We'll trim what's safe to trim and keep what matters.
When you're ready, I'll structure an offer that gives you every advantage

I read the seller's motivation, the days on market, the condition of the home, and recent comps. Then I build the offer around what's actually likely to win, not a generic template.

We're under contract. Now what?

Cue the confetti. Then we get to work. Here's what happens in the days that follow:

Day 1 – 3

Mutual Execution & Earnest Money

Once both parties sign, the contract is "executed." You'll deliver your earnest money deposit to the escrow agent named in the contract (often the settlement company) by the date in the contract. Keep it reasonable: once it's deposited, it's released only at settlement, by written agreement of both sides, or by a court.

Day 1 – 5

Loan Application Begins

Your loan application must be made by the deadline in your contract (often within 5 days of ratification). Critically: do not change jobs, finance a car, open new credit cards, or make any large purchases until after closing. Anything that touches your credit can derail the loan.

Day 3 – 10

Inspections Scheduled

We schedule inspections immediately so we have time to renegotiate or walk away before the home inspection contingency deadline written into your contract. If the home is in an HOA or condo association, you'll also get the resale disclosure packet, and Virginia law gives you a short window (3 days after you receive it) to cancel.

Day 10 – 15

Inspection Resolution & Insurance

We negotiate any inspection issues with the seller. Simultaneously, you're getting insurance quotes, since the home needs to be insurable to close.

Day 15 – 25

Appraisal & Underwriting

Lender orders the appraisal. If it comes in below offer price, we may renegotiate or you cover the gap. Underwriting works through your file.

Day 25 – 30

Clear-to-Close & Final Walk-Through

The lender issues "clear-to-close." We do a final walk-through (usually the morning of closing) to confirm the home is in the condition we agreed to.

Closing Day

Sign & Get the Keys

You wire your closing funds, sign a stack of paperwork, and walk out with the keys to your new home.

⚠ Wire Fraud Warning · Read This Twice

Wire fraud is the #1 financial threat in real estate transactions. Scammers spoof emails from settlement agents, title companies, and lenders with fake wire instructions. Before sending ANY funds:

→ Call the settlement agent at a number you've independently verified (not one from an email).
→ Confirm the routing number, account number, and recipient name verbally.
→ Never trust wire instructions sent only by email, even if they look like they came from me.

A note on AI-driven scams: Scammers now use cloned voices, fake video calls, and convincing AI-generated emails. To be unmistakably clear: I will never call, text, or email you asking for money, gift cards, or a fee of any kind, and I will never ask you to redirect or alter wiring instructions. If anyone claiming to be me asks for funds, it is not me. Hang up and call my known number.

If something feels off, stop. Call me. I'd rather we miss a deadline than lose your down payment.

Step 04 · Due Diligence

Inspections, appraisal & insurance

This is where the home reveals itself. A few hundred dollars in inspections has saved my clients tens of thousands in surprises, and given them leverage to renegotiate.

What is a home inspection, exactly?

A licensed inspector spends 2–4 hours going through every accessible system in the home: roof, foundation, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, attic, crawlspace, appliances. You get a written report (usually with photos) of everything they found.

I always recommend you attend the last 30 minutes of the inspection. The inspector will walk you through major findings in person, and you can ask questions you'd never think of from a written report.

What does "inspection period" mean?

It's the window in your contract where you have the right to inspect, request repairs or credits, or walk away with your earnest money intact. In Virginia, we write the number of days into the home inspection contingency, and you must get the inspection and deliver your repair request or termination notice before that deadline.

Virginia is a "buyer beware" state. Under the Virginia Residential Property Disclosure Act (Va. Code §55.1-700 et seq.), the seller's disclosure mostly tells you the seller makes no representations about condition, and that it's on you to investigate. That's why inspections carry more weight here.

Miss those dates and you lose your leverage, which is why we schedule everything immediately.

The home looks great. Do I really need inspections?

Yes. Always. Some of the most expensive issues (leaky roofs, foundation cracks, sewer line damage, polybutylene plumbing, knob-and-tube wiring, radon) aren't visible during a showing. A few hundred dollars now can save you tens of thousands later.

The only time I sometimes advise skipping an inspection is on a brand-new build with a strong builder warranty, and even then I usually recommend a third-party walk.

What if the inspection finds problems?

You have three main paths: ask the seller to fix it, ask for a price reduction or closing cost credit (often easier than waiting on repairs), or terminate the contract and get your earnest money back.

I'll guide you on what's reasonable to ask for, what's normal wear, and what's a deal-killer.

What is an appraisal and what if it comes in low?

The appraisal is a lender-required estimate of the home's value, done by a licensed appraiser. The lender uses it to confirm they're not lending more than the home is worth.

If it comes in low, we have options: ask the seller to lower the price, you bring extra cash to cover the gap, or split the difference. We'll talk through the math and your comfort level.

Insurance is the new inspection

Homeowners insurance has gotten harder and more expensive, especially for homes with older roofs, homes in or near a FEMA flood zone, and older Richmond homes with dated wiring or plumbing. We'll loop in an insurance agent during inspections, not after, so we can renegotiate or walk away if a home turns out to be uninsurable at a reasonable rate.

Step 05 · Closing Time

From "clear-to-close" to keys in hand

You've made it. Here's what happens in the final stretch, and what to expect on closing day.

When is the final walk-through?

Usually the day of closing or the night before. You'll walk through the home one last time to confirm it's in the agreed-upon condition, that any negotiated repairs were completed, and that nothing has changed since your last visit.

If something is off (a damaged wall, missing appliances, repairs not done), we address it before you sign. Don't skip this.

Who's at closing?

In Virginia, closing is called settlement, and it's run by a settlement agent (a real estate attorney or title company). As the buyer, you choose the settlement agent. Typically it's you, me, and the settlement agent. The seller and their agent may or may not be in the room. Many settlements are now done separately or remotely.

What will I do at closing?

Sign. A lot. Stretch your fingers. The seller signs ownership over to you, you sign your loan documents and acknowledge receipt. The whole thing usually takes 60–90 minutes.

Once it's all signed and funded, the settlement agent records the deed with the Circuit Court Clerk, and you'll get the keys (sometimes immediately, sometimes once recording is confirmed).

What should I bring?

→ A government-issued photo ID.
→ Wired closing funds (sent to the settlement agent in advance; see the wire fraud warning above).
→ A second form of ID if your closing agent requests it.
→ Patience. And maybe water.

I'll send you a closing prep email a few days before with anything specific to your transaction.

Can closing day go wrong?

Occasionally, usually due to last-minute lender issues, missing documents, or a problem flagged at the final walk-through. I prep you for the closing well in advance specifically to head these off. If something goes sideways, we usually push by a day or two. Not catastrophic.
